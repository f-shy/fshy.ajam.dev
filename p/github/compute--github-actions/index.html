Free tierOn a public repository: 'Use of the standard GitHub-hosted runners is free and unlimited on public repositories.' The billing page repeats it as 'The use of standard GitHub-hosted runners is free: In public repositories / For GitHub Pages / For Dependabot.' On a private repository the GitHub Free plan gets 2,000 minutes a month instead. No card, no phone and no identity document is required for either. The word 'unlimited' is GitHub's own, and it is qualified by the Additional Product Terms, not by the billing page: see limits.
unit unlimited; metered on wall-clock; The unlimited half is the whole finding. The private-repo 2,000 minutes is the smaller half of the same account and a different product: 2,000 minutes is 33.3 hours of wall clock.

  • retention-clamped (binding) 'If a workflow run reaches this limit, the workflow run is cancelled. This period includes execution duration, and time spent on waiting and approval.' The threshold is 35 days per workflow run, so the workflow cap is 35 days and the job cap is 6 hours; the job cap binds first. source
  • request-rate 'The shortest interval you can run scheduled workflows is once every 5 minutes.' A schedule is also only honoured on the default branch. source
  • retention-clamped (binding) 'In a public repository, scheduled workflows are automatically disabled when no repository activity has occurred in 60 days.' source
  • request-rate Job concurrency on the Free plan: 'Total concurrent jobs' 20, 'Maximum concurrent macOS jobs' 5, GPU 'Not applicable'. GitHub Support can raise it on a ticket. source

The shape, the unlimited term, the 6-hour job cap, the 35-day workflow cap, the 5-minute cron floor, the 60-day schedule disablement and the 20-job concurrency are each quoted from a GitHub page read on 2026-10-24. The 6-hour cap is the one that binds: 6 hours x 20 concurrent jobs = 120 job-hours of wall clock, or 480 vCPU-hours, in any rolling window.
Bindingretention-clamped the 6-hour job cap, not money and not concurrency: the minutes are free and unlimited on a public repository, so what fails is continuity, and a workload that must survive more than six hours needs its state outside the runner.
Identityemail (inferred, not read) 'To get started with GitHub.com, you need to have a personal account and a verified email address.' No phone: 'If you have been selected for mandatory 2FA, that does not mean you have to provide GitHub with your phone number.' No card and no hold, but this half is inferred rather than quoted, and the inference is stated: GitHub never writes 'no payment method is needed for public-repo Actions'. What the pages do say is that the card block is conditional on a quota being used up ('If your account does not have a valid payment method on file, usage is blocked once you use up your quota'), that public-repo standard runners are not billed at all, and that the always-charged products are the exception ('Larger runners are always charged for' and 'Usage of larger runners is always blocked until you set up a payment method'). A product that bills nothing has no quota to exhaust, so no card is demanded to use it. That is a documented absence, not a documented exemption, and it is one signup away from being settled.
Egressnot published: no egress quota, shaping tier or per-byte price is stated for a standard GitHub-hosted runner. Reliability note: bandwidth judged significantly excessive can lead to account suspension, throttling or limited activity; no threshold is published.
Retention'When the job begins, GitHub automatically provisions a new VM for that job. All steps in the job execute on the VM ... When the job has finished, the VM is automatically decommissioned.' Nothing on the VM survives the job. Artifacts and caches are separate services: caches are evicted after 7 days without access, capped at 10 GB per repository on GitHub Free.

NotesThe shape is confirmed exactly as the operator's answer states it: 4 vCPU, 16 GB RAM, 14 GB SSD, workflow label ubuntu-latest, on a public repository, on an email alone. Two things the phrase 'free and unlimited' hides, and both were read on a page. First, the sentence is a vendor claim on a documentation page. The meter is unlimited, but use that the vendor judges disproportionate to the benefit to the project's users, such as a content delivery network or a serverless application, can be restricted, and no threshold is published at which it is safe: a worse ceiling than a number. Second, a figure of '14 GiB SSD' understates the vendor: GitHub writes 14 GB, decimal, which is about 13.0 GiB. The record keeps the vendor's number and unit. Not established: the egress term, which no page publishes; and whether enforcement of that restriction looks like a job cancellation, a repository disablement or an account suspension in practice. That would need a probe that has not been run.
